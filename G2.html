Shadow education refers to the paid educational services that take place outside formal schooling that was made to help improve student’s academic achievement (Bray, 1999). External education has existed for centuries in many different forms, originally, shadow education was found in countries such as Japan, the Republic of Korea, and China before expanding across the world (Mori & Baker, 2010). These places have high-stakes entrance examinations which created strong demand for tutoring (Siyuan, 2021). External education rapidly expanded during the second half of the twentieth century as mass education and university participation. As educational competition increased worldwide (Mori & Baker, 2010).

As shadow education became a global phenomenon it accompanied the expansion of formal education systems (Mori & Baker, 2010). With this expansion, shadow education and mass education have been affected by social and political changes. Globalization had increased the marketization of education, the Education for All (EFA) movement, and the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) had both contributed to higher school enrolment and educational aspirations (UNESCO, 2025). These movements had successfully expanded access to education, but they had also increased the competition for academic success.

In North America, the growth of shadow education rapidly increased during the 1990s and the early 2000s. Rather than just focusing on exam preparation, these tutoring services had started to to include homework, literacy, and numeracy support. As well standardized test preparation, enrichment opportunities, and individualized learning plans (Aurini & Davies, 2004). The rise of tutoring companies such as Kumon showed the increasing demand for additional educational support outside the classrooms. According to Mori and Baker (2010), shadow education has become institutionalized along formal education. While this growth has provided many students with greater academic support, researchers raises concerns about educational equity because families with greater financial resources are in a better position to access these services.

Shadow education's impacts extend far beyond students' grades, contributing to the growing socioeconomic and educational divide.

Over the past three decades, shadow education has transformed from a support occasionally used for struggling students to an increasingly normalized part of education in North America. As private tutoring has become commercialized and more widely available, it has changed not only how students learn, but who has access to educational opportunities and how much trust the public has in the education system.

Shadow Education is seen as a resource that helps create equal opportunity by providing additional support to students in subject areas where they are struggling. However, when additional educational opportunities are available only to students whose families can afford them, this ends up doing the opposite, widening the achievement gap and reinforcing existing social advantages and inequalities.

Private tutoring is disproportionately prevalent in the highest-income and most educated areas, possibly driven by the pressures of getting into the “right university”. As schooling becomes increasingly competitive and the pressures to attend a reputable post-secondary education continue to rise, so will the opportunity gap between students with varying levels of access to resources. Fifty percent of all shadow education centres are situated in higher-income neighbourhoods with higher levels of education. Students with consistent access to tutoring may be better positioned to achieve higher grades, gain admission to competitive post-secondary programs, qualify for scholarships, and ultimately access broader career opportunities.

Currently, in British Columbia, it is estimated that half of public school students receive a form of math tutoring outside the classroom just to learn basic concepts.

This trend is leading to “the creation of a two-tiered educational system where those who have can pay for supplementary benefits, and those who don't can sink or swim” (Houle, 2018).

Some attribute the increased demand in tutoring centres to a growing lack of trust in public schools. If a child is not learning in school, parents turn to external sources and tutoring. For example, there are movements towards reforming the math curriculum in BC schools, aiming to restore proven teaching methods. When parents feel their children are not meeting grade-level requirements in the classroom, they feel pressured to compensate on their own by seeking market-based educational alternatives to support their children's learning. When parents compensate for their disappointment in the public education system through their own resources, it allows problems in BC schools to remain unaddressed. As more families view private tutoring as necessary rather than optional, parents' overall confidence in the public education system's ability to meet students' needs may continue to decline.

This change in attitude and the shift in responsibility toward parents allows educational opportunities to be distributed based on purchasing power rather than educational necessity. Rather than reducing educational inequities for those who need additional support, shadow education risks reproducing them by converting financial resources into academic advantages.

Shadow education is a term for private (almost always for profit) institutions that offer educational resources outside of school. Think private tutoring companies like Kumon or Mathnasium. They are known for their emphasis on rote memorization and one size fits all approach to learning. They also often have a tendency to focus on getting kids ahead of what they would be learning in school, instead of helping students catch up to the level they need to be at. They are becoming increasingly popular, which comes with a few issues. The biggest of which are the social inequities that stem from them, and the picture they paint of what education should look like.
