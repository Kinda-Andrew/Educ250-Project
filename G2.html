Prodigious growth in out-of-school, private educational activities meant to supplement formal schooling is observed worldwide. Why has shadow education expanded, and what does it mean for the future of education in postmodern society? Illustrated by the historical development and recent changes in the Japanese shadow education industry, it is suggested that shadow education follows the institutional logic of formal education. And as this logic has become more homogenized and expansive globally, so have the forms and goals of shadow education. Because of its focus on learning and achievement, which are central to formal education, it is predicted that shadow education will be increasingly incorporated into the broader culture of education. Shadow education refers to the paid educational services that take place outside formal schooling that was made to help improve student’s academic achievement (Bray, 1999). External education has existed for centuries in many different forms, originally, shadow education was found in countries such as Japan, the Republic of Korea, and China before expanding across the world (Mori & Baker, 2010). These places have high-stakes entrance examinations which created strong demand for tutoring (Siyuan, 2021). External education rapidly expanded during the second half of the twentieth century as mass education and university participation. As educational competition increased worldwide (Mori & Baker, 2010).

As shadow education became a global phenomenon it accompanied the expansion of formal education systems (Mori & Baker, 2010). With this expansion, shadow education and mass education have been affected by social and political changes. Globalization had increased the marketization of education, the Education for All movement, and the Sustainable Development Goals had both contributed to higher school enrolment and educational aspirations (UNESCO, 2025). These movements had successfully expanded access to education, but they had also increased the competition for academic success.

In North America, the growth of shadow education rapidly increased during the 1990s and the early 2000s. Rather than just focusing on exam preparation, these tutoring services had started to to include homework, literacy, and numeracy support. As well standardized test preparation, enrichment opportunities, and individualized learning plans (Aurini & Davies, 2004). The rise of tutoring companies such as Kumon showed the increasing demand for additional educational support outside the classrooms. According to Mori and Baker (2010), shadow education has become institutionalized along formal education. While this growth has provided many students with greater academic support, researchers raises concerns about educational equity because families with greater financial resources are in a better position to access these services.

Shadow education's impacts extend far beyond students' grades, contributing to the growing socioeconomic and educational divide.

Over the past three decades, shadow education has transformed from a support occasionally used for struggling students to an increasingly normalized part of education in North America. As private tutoring has become commercialized and more widely available, it has changed not only how students learn, but who has access to educational opportunities and how much trust the public has in the education system.

Shadow Education is seen as a resource that helps create equal opportunity by providing additional support to students in subject areas where they are struggling. However, when additional educational opportunities are available only to students whose families can afford them, this ends up doing the opposite, widening the achievement gap and reinforcing existing social advantages and inequalities.

Private tutoring is disproportionately prevalent in the highest-income and most educated areas, possibly driven by the pressures of getting into the “right university”. As schooling becomes increasingly competitive and the pressures to attend a reputable post-secondary education continue to rise, so will the opportunity gap between students with varying levels of access to resources. Fifty percent of all shadow education centres are situated in higher-income neighbourhoods with higher levels of education. Students with consistent access to tutoring may be better positioned to achieve higher grades, gain admission to competitive post-secondary programs, qualify for scholarships, and ultimately access broader career opportunities.

During the first two decades of the 21st century, the reach and influence of shadow education became all the more profound across continents. Whether provided at the home of a Myanmar student, taught in a fully packed classroom of a Greek frontistirion, or live-streamed on a Korean student’s tablet computer, private supplementary tutoring is a significant part of many students’ outside-school experiences today (Feng & Bray, 2019). Along with its growing popularity, shadow education provisions have changed shapes over the years. Students and families are now facing an increasingly industrialized tutoring sector, and in many places commercial companies’ standardised services have replaced individual tutors’ informal instructions as the dominant source of provision (Kim & Jung, 2019). In East Asia, which Manzon and Areepattamannil (2014, p. 389) described as “a cradle of private tutoring”, shadow education has long been a major phenomenon. Both high rates of participation and high levels of industrialized provision have been reported in countries such as China, Japan and South Korea (Bray, 2020; Bray & Lykins, 2012; Kim & Jung, 2019; Manzon & Areepattamannil, 2014). In these societies, industrialised supplies of shadow education have become a new normal in the 2020s, and a study about private tutoring’s developing patterns may complement the existing research literature in the field. These changes in supply have also brought challenges to the conventional conception of shadow education, which was initially used by researchers to define the types of tutoring that mimic regular schooling on academic subjects (e.g. Bray, 1999; Stevenson & Baker, 1992). In the more recent research literature, the expansion of the private tutoring industry is implicated in the expanded definitions of private supplementary tutoring and their inclusion of more diverse types of tutoring beyond the conventional “shadowing” of school education (Aurini, Davies, & Dierkes, 2013; Bray, 2017; Feng & Bray, 2019; Park, Buchmann, Choi, & Merry, 2016).

Currently, in British Columbia, it is estimated that half of public school students receive a form of math tutoring outside the classroom just to learn basic concepts.

This trend is leading to “the creation of a two-tiered educational system where those who have can pay for supplementary benefits, and those who don't can sink or swim” (Houle, 2018).

Some attribute the increased demand in tutoring centres to a growing lack of trust in public schools. If a child is not learning in school, parents turn to external sources and tutoring. For example, there are movements towards reforming the math curriculum in BC schools, aiming to restore proven teaching methods. When parents feel their children are not meeting grade-level requirements in the classroom, they feel pressured to compensate on their own by seeking market-based educational alternatives to support their children's learning. When parents compensate for their disappointment in the public education system through their own resources, it allows problems in BC schools to remain unaddressed. As more families view private tutoring as necessary rather than optional, parents' overall confidence in the public education system's ability to meet students' needs may continue to decline.

This change in attitude and the shift in responsibility toward parents allows educational opportunities to be distributed based on purchasing power rather than educational necessity. Rather than reducing educational inequities for those who need additional support, shadow education risks reproducing them by converting financial resources into academic advantages.

In the Mao era, a modern education system was eventually established during the 1950s and 1960s following the Soviet model (Gu, 2004; Walder, 2015). Like many of his other ideas, Mao’s education policies were largely influenced by his radical egalitarian ideals of socialism. Especially during the Cultural Revolution, Mao pushed for a “redness” doctrine which viewed education as a device to convey Marxist-Leninist revolutionary values and focused on educating the lower class of workers and peasants (Pepper, 2000; Walder, 2015). Following this direction, less priority was given to higher education. Rather, programmes with part-work part-study schemes for the alliance of workers and peasants were prioritised, which “aimed at achieving mass literacy, providing local initiative, expanding rural educational opportunities, [and] encouraging curricular and structural innovation” (Saywell, 1980, p. 2). Mao also ordered the abolition of school entrance examinations, which had long been a key component of education in China (Andreas, 2004). The new admission system heavily stressed the candidates’ class backgrounds and political loyalties. For example, high school graduates had to work for one or two years before they could be recommended by their own work units to enter universities (Pepper, 2000, p. 384; Saywell, 1980).

Shadow education is a term for private (almost always for profit) institutions that offer educational resources outside of school. Think private tutoring companies like Kumon or Mathnasium. They are known for their emphasis on rote memorization and one size fits all approach to learning. They also often have a tendency to focus on getting kids ahead of what they would be learning in school, instead of helping students catch up to the level they need to be at. They are becoming increasingly popular, which comes with a few issues. The biggest of which are the social inequities that stem from them, and the picture they paint of what education should look like.
